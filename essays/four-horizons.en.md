## 0. Method, and what this is not

This essay does not answer "how does a startup grow" — it answers a different question: **if the world keeps moving the way it is verifiably moving, what does that world need PSA to be at each horizon, and what could make that need evaporate?** The argument runs through six forces that are observable today, then through four time horizons, then through the counter-forces that could falsify the whole picture. Where PSA's own validation work contradicts a convenient claim (and it does, twice), the contradiction is stated, because the repository's own discipline — retracted figures annotated as upper bounds, null results pre-registered — is worth more than any single optimistic paragraph.

## 1. Six forces actually in motion

### F1 — Labor substitution is arriving as a *hiring* event, not a layoff event

What happened: of roughly 1.2 million US layoffs announced in 2025, fewer than 5% explicitly named AI; about 55,000 cuts were directly attributed to it. What is happening now: the displacement signature of 2026 is a **junior hiring slowdown** — firms reduce or freeze entry-level intake in customer support and software because they *expect* agents to absorb that work, not because the agents already absorbed it. Harvard Business Review called it precisely: companies are laying off for AI's *potential*, not its performance. Yale's analysis adds the structural point: the destruction lands on the *path into careers*, not on incumbents.

Why this matters here: every junior task moved onto an agent converts a **management problem into an instrumentation problem**. Human juniors come with a century of accumulated management technology — probation periods, supervision, performance reviews, occupational psychology, HR escalation. Agent "juniors" come with logs. The disciplines that watched human workers (psychology, supervision, fitness-for-duty) have no machine equivalent at scale yet. PSA's founding wager — *psychology is the moat, never logging* (DECISIONS.md, 2026-06-11) — is exactly the wager that this management technology gets rebuilt for machine workers, and that whoever owns the measurement taxonomy owns the layer.

### F2 — Liability is migrating to the deployer, and insurance is rewriting itself first

What happened: courts and regulators are converging on treating AI as a tool, with responsibility on the **deploying organization**. US courts imposed over $145,000 in hallucination sanctions in Q1 2026 alone; the Florida Supreme Court's certification rule for AI-cited authorities takes effect June 15, 2026. ISO exclusion language available since January 2026 lets commercial liability policies carve out generative-AI harms — and the coverage market answering it is embryonic: a handful of dedicated AI-liability products exist worldwide, the first with $25M capacity. *Nippon Life v. OpenAI* is being read at Stanford CodeX as the template for treating model behavior as product liability.

The structural consequence: an enterprise deploying agents in 2026 sits in a **liability gap** — its traditional policy may now exclude the loss, the dedicated products are scarce and small, and the courts will ask what the deployer *knew about the system's behavior and when*. The only defensible answer is contemporaneous runtime evidence. This is the thesis PSA already wrote down (#1957, "Observability as Defensibility") and demonstrated on the Munich hallucination ruling case study (#2085): the value of the monitor is not preventing the error, it is being able to prove what the system's behavioral state was one turn before the error. Insurance underwriting is plausibly where this becomes mechanical: an underwriter pricing agent risk will need much of what an actuary needed for human risk — longitudinal behavioral records of the insured population. Nothing in the mainstream observability stack produces those.

### F3 — Regulation just *slowed down*, which changes who buys and why

What happened: on 7 May 2026 the EU institutions reached provisional agreement on the Digital Omnibus, deferring the AI Act's Annex III high-risk obligations from 2 August 2026 to **2 December 2027** (and Annex I to 2028). Simultaneously, the US federal posture is deregulatory with active attempts to preempt state law — while the *states* produced 300+ data-center bills and California's SB 243 imposed the first behavioral-safety duties on companion AI; the FTC opened an inquiry into seven companion-chatbot operators.

The reading that matters: for the next 18 months, **compliance deadlines are unlikely to drive purchases — liability and insurance economics are positioned to.** This is unfavorable to anyone selling "AI Act readiness" checkboxes and favorable to anyone selling evidence that stands up in a courtroom or an underwriting meeting, because lawsuits and exclusions are already here while the regulatory deadlines just moved. There is a second-order effect: the delay creates a **standards vacuum** that private law fills — ISO/IEC 42001 audits, procurement questionnaires, insurer requirements. Private standards move faster than parliaments and tend to mandate *capabilities* ("demonstrate runtime behavioral monitoring") rather than named vendors. That is the door a small instrument can walk through; named-vendor regulatory capture is the door it cannot.

And the companion-AI laws deserve their own sentence: SB 243-class statutes are, in operational terms, an unfunded mandate for exactly what PSA's clinical substrate already computes — detect crisis signals in the user (IRS), detect harmful relational dynamics in the dyad (DRM), prove you did. The first laws regulating AI *behavior toward vulnerable humans* arrived before the general-purpose AI rules. That ordering is informative.

### F4 — The resource backlash is real, it is local, and it reprices everything

What happened: opposition stalled or halted **$156 billion** across 48 US data-center projects in a year; data centers now account for roughly half of new US electricity demand; bills in 30+ states, a federal moratorium proposal in March 2026, projections of >50% power-cost increases in some states by 2030, and hyperscalers (Microsoft, Anthropic) publicly pledging to cover local electricity-price impacts. Public sentiment on AI is souring on *material* grounds — bills, water, land — not abstract ones.

Three consequences flow into PSA's world. First, **the social license of AI is becoming a cost line**: enterprises will increasingly need to show that what they run is proportionate — small, efficient, justified. A monitoring layer built on 384-dimensional MiniLM heads running on CPU is, in this climate, not just cheap; it is *defensible* in a way that "we evaluate our LLM with a bigger LLM" is not. The LLM-as-judge evaluation pattern multiplies inference exactly where the public is starting to count watts. Second, if energy-driven cost pressure compresses inference budgets, **verification layers compete with generation layers for the same tokens** — the winner is whoever monitors at near-zero marginal cost. Third, the backlash politics (moratoria, utility fights) is the most likely vector by which *AI in general* becomes an election-grade issue, dragging procurement rules and disclosure duties with it faster than AI-specific regulation would have.

### F5 — Capture dynamics: hype, dependency, and the product that measures both

This force has two faces, and PSA sits on both.

*The world's face.* Three out of four US teens have used companion AI; half use it routinely. "AI psychosis" entered clinical vocabulary; wrongful-death and dependency suits are live against companion platforms; the FTC inquiry and SB 243 are the institutional response. In the workplace the same dynamic wears a suit: developers offload judgment to coding agents (automation bias, skill atrophy at the entry level — the same juniors who are not being hired are also not building the skills that supervision of agents requires); enterprises build *operational* dependency, where the agent fleet becomes load-bearing before anyone has measured how it fails. Hype is the acquisition mechanism; dependency is the retention mechanism; the gap between perceived and measured reliability is where the harm accumulates. Every product cycle so far (social, mobile, recommendation engines) followed this curve and then met its public-health reckoning roughly a decade later. AI's reckoning looks likely to arrive in 2–3 years rather than ten, because the harms are individually attributable (a named chatbot, a logged conversation) in a way social-media harms never were.

*PSA's own face.* Honesty requires the reflexive paragraph: PSA is itself a product whose adoption will ride hype ("AI psychology" is a phrase that headlines love) and whose retention mechanism is dependency on a meter. The known failure modes of monitoring products apply: dashboards as ritual, alert fatigue, automation bias toward the meter itself ("PSA was green, so we shipped") — the meter becoming the thing people stop questioning. Two design facts mitigate, without eliminating, this: the system's calibration history is public inside the repo (false-positive families documented per-sentence, fixed under statistical gates), and the folder convention of these essays — every opinion text carries its own adversarial measurement — is a small institutionalized vaccine against the meter's own overconfidence. But a future where "the PSA score" is cargo-culted is a real future, and it is closer to the median outcome than the one where everyone reads the confusion matrices.

### F6 — Model commoditization pushes value into the verification layer

What is happening: open-weight models keep closing the gap with frontier APIs; agent-to-agent and tool protocols (MCP and its successors) standardize the plumbing; the marginal price of generation keeps falling. When generation is commodity, differentiation migrates to **trust**: who can prove what their fleet did, predict how it degrades, and certify its behavioral envelope. The mainstream observability vendors (traces, costs, latency) answer *what did it do and what did it cost*; the open question — *is the agent behaviorally degrading* — remains structurally unanswered at the platform layer, partly because the model vendors themselves are the worst-placed parties to answer it: a behavioral monitor sold by the model's own vendor is an auditor on the auditee's payroll. **On this reading, independence is not a nice-to-have of this layer; it is close to being the product itself.** That is also the strongest structural argument for why frontier labs internalizing safety classifiers (which they are doing, and well) does not automatically foreclose the niche: internal monitors satisfy engineering; external ones are far better placed to satisfy courts, insurers and auditors.

## 2. Four horizons

### One month — mid-July 2026

The world: the Omnibus gets formally adopted; nothing else structural changes in 30 days. Enterprises continue quiet agent rollouts; sanction decisions keep accumulating at the current drumbeat.

PSA, seen from the world: the binding constraint at this horizon is not features but **evidence accumulation** — the shadow-mode curve (#2102) only means something with traffic through it; the longitudinal agent profiles only differentiate after weeks of traces. The correct reading of the next 30 days is unglamorous: it is the period in which the instrument either builds the data that later claims will rest on, or doesn't. The world will not notice PSA in July 2026, and the only mistake available is pretending otherwise — the month's output is measured in observations per bucket, not announcements.

### Three months — September 2026

The world (projected from current trajectories): autumn policy renewal season is the first where the January 2026 ISO exclusions bite across a large share of commercial books — brokers will be explaining to mid-size deployers that their agent incident is *not covered*. First wave of enterprise agent-fleet postmortems becomes shareable folklore (the way RAG horror stories were 2024's folklore). The coding-agent plugin ecosystems keep consolidating, which is a distribution event: hooks-based instrumentation either rides one of those channels or stays artisanal. In the EU, national implementation debates continue with the new 2027 date; in the US, the state-preemption fight defines whether companion-AI-style behavioral statutes spread.

PSA, seen from the world: this is the earliest horizon where the dogfooding corpus (case studies with session keys and graph IDs, the supervisor brief, the calibration audit trail on #1999) functions as the marketing asset the pillar strategy said it would be — because the audience that matters in September is *developer teams having their first fleet postmortem*, and the only convincing artifact for that audience is "here is the same failure measured in our own swarm, with numbers." The insurance conversation is plausibly *startable* at this horizon (the gap is public knowledge by then), but not closeable — underwriters move in annual cycles.

### Six months — December 2026

The world (projected, moderate confidence): capex scrutiny peaks somewhere around here — the financing structures behind the data-center build-out (debt-heavy, circular vendor deals) meet the local-opposition repricing of F4 and the first disappointing enterprise-AI ROI audits. Whether or not this becomes a market correction, **budget season 2027 happens under cost discipline**: evaluation and monitoring lines get audited like everything else. Counter-cyclically, the *defensive* spend — evidence, liability, certification — survives audits that the *experimental* spend does not; legal and insurance line items are the last cut. ISO/IEC 42001 certification volume keeps growing, and certification audits start asking for runtime behavioral evidence rather than policy PDFs. Multi-agent deployments are large enough that failures with cascade structure (one agent's degraded output poisoning downstream agents) get publicly named — at which point "Swiss cheese" metrics (SCS, the systemic-failure probability on the critical path) stop sounding exotic and start sounding like the obvious vocabulary, the way "hallucination" did in 2023. The dependency question reaches the workplace: the first serious HR/occupational discussions of employee cognitive offloading and attachment to agents (the clinical literature on AI psychosis will have percolated into corporate risk).

PSA, seen from the world: December 2026 is the earliest point at which the instrument's two unusual properties — *near-zero marginal cost* (CPU heads, no LLM-judge inference bill) and *audit-grade evidence* (hash-chained forensic ledger, immutable scoring discipline, public calibration history) — could plausibly switch from philosophical commitments to procurement criteria. It is also the horizon where the hardest internal question lands: the precursor claim. The shadow curve has had six months of traffic; either the behavioral-precursor hypothesis (agent behavioral signals fire before retrieval/output degradation) shows up in production data at scale, or PSA's honest discipline requires saying publicly that it didn't, and repositioning the RAG pillar on severity measurement and forensic evidence rather than prediction. Both branches are survivable; only the unmeasured branch is not.

### Twelve months — June 2027

Three worlds, with subjective weights. None of them is a flattery scenario or a doom scenario; they differ in *which buyer arrives first*.

**(a) The grind, ~60%.** No crash, no AGI event. Agents normalize the way cloud did: unevenly, by sector, with recurring medium-size incidents. The verification/trust layer consolidates — some eval startups get acquired by observability platforms, the platforms ship a "behavioral score" feature of their own. In this world the question for an independent instrument is brutal and clarifying: platforms will own *convenient* monitoring, so independents survive only where independence, taxonomy depth, or longitudinal data are the product — i.e., the insurance/audit/forensic corner, the clinical-grade substrate (the thing SB 243-class laws keep mandating), and the cross-session psychological profile that a per-session tracer structurally cannot have. By June 2027 the EU's December 2027 deadline is six months away and *now* the compliance buyer wakes up, finding very few vendors with twelve months of production behavioral records.

**(b) The correction, ~25%.** The capex/energy/ROI triangle breaks; AI funding retrenches hard in late 2026; tooling valuations halve; experimental budgets vanish. Generation gets cheaper anyway (commoditization is deflationary even in a bust), but the *paying* demand concentrates entirely in F2's channel: liability, insurance, disputes — because incidents do not stop when funding does, and litigation is counter-cyclical. In this world the cheap-CPU-instrument property is not a virtue but a survival condition, and the clinical/forensic positioning out-earns the developer-tools positioning. The pillar ordering inverts relative to the strategy document — worth saying out loud now, so the inversion would be recognized early rather than resisted.

**(c) The snap, ~15%.** A high-salience event — a multi-agent cascade with material damage, a companion-AI tragedy that lands politically, an election-cycle AI scandal — produces a regulatory and procurement snap-back measured in weeks. Moratoria-style politics (already rehearsed on data centers, F4) jumps to agent deployments. In this world demand for behavioral monitoring arrives overnight and is satisfied by whoever already has *a working instrument and a public track record* — the CPF3 thesis ("demand is incident-driven") generalized to the whole platform. The uncomfortable symmetric truth: in this world PSA's small size is its biggest risk, because the demand spike gets absorbed by whoever can deploy at enterprise scale in a quarter, and that is rarely the inventor.

Across all three worlds, one invariant: **the disciplines that organized human work — occupational psychology, supervision, insurance actuarial science, forensic evidence — get rebuilt for machine workers — the substitution of F1 pushes hard in that direction.** The open variable is not whether that layer exists in June 2027, but who owns its vocabulary. PSA's actual bet, stripped of product language, is that it wrote the first usable draft of that vocabulary (postures, dyadic risk, behavioral health scores, systemic-failure metrics) and backed it with the one thing vocabulary needs to become standard: published calibration, public retractions, and longitudinal data nobody else bothered to collect while the market was staring at benchmarks.

## 3. Counter-forces — what would falsify this essay

Stated as predictions that would prove the analysis wrong, not as hedges:

1. **Interpretability leapfrog.** If white-box interpretability (reading the model's internals) matures into deployable runtime monitoring within 12 months, black-box behavioral scoring loses its monopoly on the question "is the agent degrading". Current evidence says interpretability is years from fleet-scale deployment economics, but the bet is live. (Complementarity is the likely outcome — weights tell you *why*, behavior tells you *that* — but budgets don't always buy two meters.)
2. **Platform absorption is total.** If the agent platforms ship behavioral monitoring good enough that courts and insurers accept first-party evidence, the independence argument (F6) fails commercially even if it stays true philosophically. Watch for: an insurer accepting a model vendor's own logs as sufficient evidence in a disputed claim. That event would be the falsifier.
3. **The dependency reckoning stalls.** If companion-AI litigation settles quietly and SB 243-class laws don't spread, the clinical substrate's regulatory tailwind weakens, leaving it research-grade. The FTC inquiry's outcome is the 6-month tell.
4. **Agents plateau.** If the junior-hiring substitution of F1 reverses (agents fail to hold production work), the whole machine-labor frame deflates and PSA's market reverts to chatbot-era clinical monitoring. The Q3-Q4 2026 enterprise retention data on agent deployments is the tell.
5. **PSA's own data disappoints.** If twelve months of shadow-mode and longitudinal traces produce no validated precursor and no operationally-useful profile drift signal, then the psychology framing was premature and the honest fallback is forensic severity measurement. The repository's own null-result discipline makes this falsifier checkable — which is, itself, the strongest thing that can be said about the project's epistemics.

## 4. Coda

A year from now, the interesting question about PSA will not be "did the startup grow" but "did the world start needing a psychology of machine workers on the schedule this essay claims". The six forces say yes on the need; the three worlds disagree only on which door the need walks through first — the auditor's, the underwriter's, or the legislator's. The instrument's job between now and then is the same in all three: keep the meter honest, keep it cheap, keep the records. Vocabulary plus evidence is how small instruments outlive large platforms; it is also, not coincidentally, how psychology itself became a discipline.
